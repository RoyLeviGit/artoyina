## ACT 1: FOUR PAINTINGS, ONE LABEL

*[READ: Brisk, light, curious; give each image a beat.]*

[ARTWORK: Seurat, *A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte*]

[TALKING HEAD]

Look at this painting. Thousands of tiny dots of color, all carefully arranged.

[ARTWORK: Cézanne, *Les Grandes Baigneuses*]

Now this one. big blocks of color, with bodies and trees shaped to fit the composition.

[ARTWORK: Van Gogh, *The Red Vineyard*]

Here, the brushstrokes are bold, and the colors feel more intense than real life.

[ARTWORK: Gauguin, *Mahana no atua (Day of the God)*]

And here, large, flat areas of color create a scene that feels half real, half imagined.

*[READ: Ease the pace; conversational, gently puzzled.]*

These paintings look very different, yet art history puts all four under the same name: Post-Impressionism. To understand why, we need to start with what they had in common: the influence of Impressionism.

*[READ: Steady, clear, enthusiastic; a friendly recap.]*

The Impressionists painted everyday subjects with bright colors and visible brushstrokes. They were interested in how things looked under changing light, so every painting became a study of the shades and colors of a particular moment — pushing artists to look deeply, paint quickly, and use bold brushwork. That expressiveness appealed to the artists we’re looking at today. But each wanted to use it for something different.

*[READ: Measured, reassuring; make the distinction feel simple.]*

That is why Post-Impressionism can be a confusing name. These artists weren't members of one group with a shared style or manifesto. The British critic Roger Fry introduced the label in 1910, looking back at the different directions painting had taken after Impressionism. Those experiments overlapped with Impressionism too. Monet and his fellow artists were still working while these new approaches developed.

*[READ: Brisk, crisp, engaged; distinguish each approach.]*

Seurat turned color into a system, while Cézanne used it to build structure and weight. Van Gogh used it to express emotion, and Gauguin combined what he saw with memory, belief, and imagination.

*[READ: Slower, confident; one beat for each word.]*

System. Structure. Emotion. Dream.

*[READ: Natural pace, inviting; move the story forward.]*

We'll start with Seurat and the painting we saw first. He exhibited it alongside the Impressionists at their final group show in 1886. It is a good place to begin because the subject looks familiar, but his way of painting it makes the whole scene feel different.

## ACT 2: SEURAT — COLOR BECOMES A SYSTEM

[TALKING HEAD]

*[READ: Steady, precise, curious; introduce the person warmly.]*

Georges Seurat was a young French painter, born in Paris in 1859. He had studied traditional drawing and composition, but he was also interested in scientific theories about color. He wanted to bring those two interests together: the brightness of Impressionism and a carefully planned way of building a picture.

[ARTWORK: Seurat, *A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte*, full screen]

*[READ: Measured, observant; let curiosity build toward the close-up.]*

He began *La Grande Jatte* in 1884, when he was twenty-four, and worked on it for about two years. The subject is very Impressionist: Parisians relaxing beside the Seine. But instead of making the scene feel like a passing moment, he makes the figures seem almost fixed in place. To see how, we need to look at the painting both up close and from afar.

[GRAPHIC: Extreme close-up of painted surface. Slowly pull back.]

*[READ: Slow, focused, quietly fascinated; let us inspect the dots.]*

When we get close, the surface breaks into small touches of different colors. Seurat placed contrasting colors beside each other so they would interact when seen from a distance. He used research by Michel Eugène Chevreul, a French chemist who studied how neighboring colors affect one another's appearance. (I talked about him in my previous video.)
Seurat wanted to make these effects something he could plan and control. Unlike the Impressionists, who often used these effects to capture how light and color behaved in a specific moment, Seurat didn’t copy nature as it is — he organized it.

*[READ: Measured, clear, lightly playful; give the long word room.]*

The separation of colors is called Divisionism, also called chromoluminarism.

*[READ: Quick, dry aside; lightly amused.]*

Yes, I only mentioned that to try saying that word. Painting with small dots is called Pointillism.

*[READ: Slow into the pun; deadpan, playfully self-aware.]*

The point...

*[SFX: Brief comic drum sting.]*

*[READ: Resume naturally; composed, with a trace of amusement.]*

...was to make color more systematic, and that control extended beyond the brushstrokes.

[ARTWORK DETAIL: Figures, parasol, tree trunks, woman with monkey]

*[READ: Slow, observant, slightly unsettled; let the stillness register.]*

Look at the repeated verticals: trees, figures, umbrellas. They bring an unnatural stillness to the composition. Different social classes share the same space, but barely interact. They are scattered in a way that looks half natural, half artificial, separating them into isolated groups. The carefully arranged figures help explain why such a bright outdoor scene can feel stiff and distant.

[SIDE BY SIDE: Monet landscape or leisure scene / Seurat, *La Grande Jatte*]

*[READ: Pick up slightly; clear, animated by the contrast.]*

Compare that with Monet. His loose brushstrokes make light and movement feel alive. Seurat uses separate dots of color to create something more planned and controlled. So the dots are only part of the change. Seurat takes an everyday outing and turns it into a deliberate arrangement in which color and composition shape how we experience the people.

That’s how he developed Impressionist ideas: using their experiments with color and visible brushstrokes to create something planned, rather than capturing a passing moment.

*[READ: Slower, subdued, matter-of-fact; pause after his age.]*

Unfortunately, his career was very short. He died suddenly in 1891, at just thirty-one. But in his few years as an artist, he left a mark on art history and showed one possible path beyond Impressionism.

*[READ: Return to a natural pace; warm, curious about the next problem.]*

Our next Post-Impressionist artist is Cézanne. He was also searching for order, but through a different problem: how to keep Impressionist color while making objects feel solid and substantial.

## ACT 3: CÉZANNE — BUILDING WITH COLOR

[TALKING HEAD]

*[READ: Easy, conversational, warm; settle into his story.]*

Paul Cézanne was born in Aix-en-Provence in 1839. His banker father wanted him to study law, but he left for Paris to become a painter. There, he met Camille Pissarro, an Impressionist who encouraged him to paint outdoors and use lighter colors. I mentioned Pissarro briefly in my video on Impressionism. He was an important mentor to many painters of his time.

*[READ: Measured, grounded, thoughtful; give his aim some weight.]*

Cézanne exhibited with the Impressionists, but gradually took their approach in his own direction. He shared their interest in color and visible brushstrokes, but wanted to use them to build solid forms and balanced compositions. He simplified reality, experimented with perspective, and carefully studied nature. This is his place in Post-Impressionism: turning Impressionist techniques into a more structured and personal way of painting.

[SIDE BY SIDE: Three versions of *Mont Sainte-Victoire*]

*[READ: Slow, patient, absorbed; leave room to compare the views.]*

Look at these paintings of Mont Sainte-Victoire, the mountain near his home. Cézanne returned to it over many years, changing his viewpoint and the way he painted it. He didn’t simply copy the landscape. Instead, he experimented with deliberate brushstrokes and carefully chosen colors. The familiar view became a way to test how color could build a landscape without carefully outlining everything.

[ARTWORK: Cézanne, *The Card Players*]

He took this idea of simplifying reality and manipulating color and composition even further in his later paintings.

*[READ: Slow, precise, curious; guide the eye from object to object.]*

In The Card Players, the man, table, and bottle are all tilted together, aligned at similar angles in a composition that isn’t entirely realistic.


[ARTWORK: Cézanne, *The Large Bathers*]

*[READ: Keep it slow; open, quietly impressed by the whole arrangement.]*

In *The Large Bathers*, the bodies and trees form a triangle. Faces are barely described, and the figures bend to fit the larger arrangement. We notice how the whole painting holds together before we notice any individual person.


*[READ: Pick up slightly; assured, appreciative of his influence.]*

Cézanne’s experiments with color and composition helped open the way for later art movements.
His way of breaking subjects into connected shapes and experimenting with different viewpoints influenced later artists like Picasso and Matisse. Cézanne stayed relatively close to nature, but showed younger artists how freely they could rebuild it.


*[READ: Conversational, reflective; slow and soften at “But what if”.]*

So Cézanne and Seurat took the Impressionists’ expressive color and brushwork and pushed them toward experiments with structure and style. But what if that same freedom was used not to rebuild the outside world, but to express what was happening inside your soul? One of the most famous and remarkable examples is Van Gogh.

## ACT 4: VAN GOGH — COLOR BECOMES EMOTION

[TALKING HEAD]

*[READ: Steady, warm, attentive; introduce the artistic change clearly.]*

Van Gogh's connection to Post-Impressionism begins with what Impressionism gave him: brighter colors and separate, visible brushstrokes. He used those techniques increasingly to express a feeling about a subject. We can follow that change through his paintings, from the dark scenes he made in the Netherlands to the intense colors of his years in France.

*[READ: Ease down; personal, empathetic, understated.]*

He chose painting at twenty-seven, after several other careers hadn't worked out. His brother Theo supported him financially and emotionally, and their letters show what he wanted his art to communicate.

Two years after choosing art, he wrote to Theo:

*[READ: Slow, intimate, vulnerable; grow quietly firm at the end.]*

"What am I in the eyes of most people — a nonentity, an eccentric, or an unpleasant person — somebody who has no position in society and will never have one; in short, the lowest of the low. All right, then — even if that were absolutely true, then I should one day like to show by my work what such an eccentric, such a nobody, has in his heart."

### From Brown to Color

*[READ: Return to a natural pace; warm, gently explanatory.]*

The wish to express something personal was there before he encountered Impressionism. Let’s have a peek at one of his earlier paintings.

[ARTWORK: Van Gogh, *The Potato Eaters*]

*[READ: Slow, grounded, empathetic; give the hands and faces time.]*

In *The Potato Eaters*, five peasants sit beneath a weak lamp. The dark colors, worn faces, and rough hands emphasize the hard labor behind the meal. Even before Impressionism, Van Gogh was using style and color to create a certain feeling. After encountering it, he would push this approach further and further.

[ARTWORK: Van Gogh, *Self-Portrait*, 1887, Van Gogh Museum; the short-stroke portrait discussed by MFA Houston below]

*[READ: Brighter, slightly quicker; let the discovery feel fresh.]*

When he moved to Paris in 1886, he discovered the brighter colors of Impressionism and the experiments of artists such as Seurat.

In this self-portrait, you can clearly see the influence of Impressionism, but Van Gogh adapts it to his own artistic interests.


*[READ: Ease down; searching, quietly unsettled.]*

His colors and brushstrokes create the emotional tone of the painting — an unstable feeling, a kind of hum from a busy, unsettled mind.

*[READ: Unhurried, personal, wondering; leave a beat after the question.]*

Can you look at this painting and not feel a little shaken by it? To me, this really feels like a deeply emotional piece.



### Arles

*[READ: Natural pace, warm, hopeful; a light aside on “Yep”.]*

In Arles, in 1888, Van Gogh hoped to create a shared home and studio for artists. He invited Gauguin to join him. Yep, the same Gauguin we introduced at the beginning. We’ll talk about him in the next part.

[ARTWORK: Van Gogh, *The Bedroom*]
[ARTWORK: Van Gogh, *The Night Café*]
[ARTWORK: Van Gogh, *Café Terrace at Night*]

Around this time, Van Gogh painted some of his most famous works, including Café Terrace at Night, The Night Café, and The Bedroom.

*[READ: Measured, engaged; turn gently uneasy with the café.]*

He used intense colors, strong contrasts, and distorted perspectives to create very different moods —from the warmth and excitement of the night to the uneasy, almost threatening atmosphere of the café. Even The Bedroom, a simple and familiar space, feels strange, unstable. These paintings show how Van Gogh was becoming more experimental with color and shape, using them to change reality and express emotion.


### Finding Some Calm

*[READ: Unhurried, plain, compassionate; lower the energy.]*

When Gauguin arrived in October 1888, the two artists disagreed about how far that experimentation should go. Van Gogh usually needed a subject in front of him; Gauguin encouraged working from memory and imagination. After nine tense weeks together, Van Gogh experienced a serious mental health crisis and injured his ear. Gauguin left. 

The following May, Van Gogh voluntarily entered the asylum at Saint-Rémy, hoping to find some calm. Between periods of deep distress, he continued painting, bringing observation and imagination together.

*[Music drops out.]*

[ARTWORK: Van Gogh, *The Starry Night*, full screen. Hold in silence.]

*[READ: Unhurried, gentle, attentive; emerge softly from the silence.]*

*The Starry Night* began with the view from his window, but he obviously filled it with imagination. To get into the technical details, the village wasn't visible from that window, and he moved the tree to serve the composition. He was putting the scene together, not simply recording it.

*[READ: Conversational, intimate, sincere; slow at “really look”.]*

I know this painting is incredibly famous, and sometimes that can make it feel almost cliché. But let's take a moment to really look at it without any preconceptions, because it really is hypnotically beautiful.

*[READ: Slow, hushed wonder; let the rhythm flow with the sky.]*

Look at the contrast between the small, quiet houses and the broad curves filling the sky. The dark tree links the ground to the sky, creating a continuous flow between earth and air. The tree leads into the swirls in the sky, flowing into the mountains that spill toward the village and back to the tree. Rings of light make the moon and stars seem to pulse. Everything moves, shines, flows.

The bold brushstrokes he developed after encountering Impressionism guide us through the whole scene, which is shaped by memory and feeling.

*[READ: Pause for three beats; let the image linger.]*


### What His Work Leaves Us

*[READ: Slow, plain, compassionate; pause after the final sentence.]*

Van Gogh left the asylum in May 1890 and died two months later, at thirty-seven. His death is generally understood as suicide.

*[READ: Measured, grounded, respectful; lift gently toward his work.]*

Despite struggling with mental health throughout his life, Van Gogh painted at an extraordinary pace and created an impressive number of paintings in his short life.

*[READ: Steady, assured, appreciative of his artistic choices.]*

His deliberate use of color, form, and expressive brushwork helped later artists, including the Expressionists and Fauves, find new ways to communicate emotion.



*[READ: Natural pace, composed, curious; gently reset.]*

For our last example of Post-Impressionism, let’s go back to the man from the disagreement in Arles: Gauguin.

*[READ: Ease down; sober, direct; let the final clause land.]*

For him, painting from memory and imagination was a central part of his art — and he didn’t always take it to the right places.

## ACT 5: GAUGUIN — PAINTING FROM MEMORY

[TALKING HEAD]

*[READ: Steady, clear, curious; focus on how the paintings work.]*

Gauguin took Impressionist influence in another direction. Where Van Gogh intensified a scene to express feeling, Gauguin increasingly painted from memory, mixing what he had seen with what he imagined. That shift toward invented color and symbolic scenes is what places him in this Post-Impressionist story.

*[READ: Conversational, lightly wry; sober up at “left his family”.]*

He started painting while working as a stockbroker, married with five children. He collected Impressionist paintings, exhibited with the group, and learned from everyone's mentor, our dude Pissarro. After the financial crash of 1882, he turned to art full-time and eventually left his family behind.

*[READ: Brief, dry, disapproving; let it land.]*

Not cool.

*[READ: Measured, serious, disapproving; keep the ending level.]*

And unfortunately, this is only the beginning of his controversial life choices — you’ll see what I mean in a second...

*[READ: Natural pace, composed, interested; return to the setting.]*

Looking for a cheaper life away from Paris, Gauguin went to Brittany in 1886. Its rural life and religious traditions attracted him, and he began turning these subjects into paintings.

### Vision After the Sermon

[ARTWORK: Gauguin, *Vision After the Sermon*]

*[READ: Slow, absorbed, curious; let the strange scene unfold.]*

In *Vision After the Sermon*, women from Brittany appear to witness the biblical story of Jacob wrestling an angel. A tree trunk cuts across the canvas, dividing the praying women from the struggle. Yet both occupy the same bright red ground, so an imagined event seems as present as the people watching it.

[SIDE BY SIDE: Impressionist outdoor scene / *Vision After the Sermon*]

*[READ: Steady, clear, engaged; emphasize what the red changes.]*

An Impressionist might use color to show light falling across a field. Gauguin uses this red to take the scene outside everyday experience. Repeated white caps, dark dresses, and heavy outlines turn the figures into flat shapes, reducing the depth that would make the scene look natural.

*[READ: Measured, questioning; stress “his”, then turn sober.]*

So Gauguin has taken the Impressionists’ freedom with color and used it to paint belief. But this is *his* image of the faith of women from Brittany, not a direct record of their experience. That distinction becomes harder to ignore when he leaves Europe for a very different world...

### Tahiti

*[READ: Pick up slightly; curious, with restrained anticipation.]*

By 1891, Gauguin wanted a bigger escape. Brittany had offered subjects that seemed distant from modern Paris, but his career was still struggling. He sailed to Tahiti hoping to find a different life and a new source for his art — a place he believed was untouched by European civilization.

*[READ: Slower, firm, sober; pause after “a French colony”.]*

But that vision didn’t match reality. Tahiti was already a French colony, shaped by European influence. Still, it became the world of his paintings. He combined local people and places with myths and scenes he invented, using the same freedom with color and imagination we’d seen in Brittany.
[ARTWORK: Gauguin, *Spirit of the Dead Watching*]

*[READ: Slow, restrained, concerned; keep the description plain.]*

In *Spirit of the Dead Watching*, a young girl lies awake on a bed, looking toward us, while a dark figure sits behind her. Gauguin said she feared a spirit. Yet her gaze leaves the source of that fear uncertain: she isn't looking at the figure behind her.

*[READ: Slow, level, grave; pause after her age.]*

It is generally believed that this girl was only thirteen, and that Gauguin was in an intimate relationship with her. That gives us a darker, deeper understanding of his character — he repeatedly placed his desires and imagination above the people he depicted.

*[READ: Measured, firm, reflective; emphasize “his” and “hers”.]*

This painting shows *his* explanation of her feelings, not hers. As in the painting from Brittany, he gives us an imagined inner world. But here, it may tell us more about the painter than the person he depicts.

[ARTWORK: Gauguin, *Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going?*]

*[READ: Unhurried, composed, attentive; give each title question space.]*

He expanded this imagined world in *Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going?*, painted in 1897. 

Scale and perspective shift, as the warm bodies stand out against the blue-green setting, connecting the figures who don't all seem to share the same moment. 

Rather than showing one event, Gauguin has arranged the characters around the questions in the title. 

*[READ: Slow, contemplative; leave a beat between the life stages.]*

Read from right to left, the Tahitian figures suggest birth, life, and death. 

*[READ: Measured, sober, questioning; let the qualification register.]*

The question has become universal, but the people used to ask it are still filtered through his fantasy of Polynesia. 

The painting gives us no clear answers. 

This is his move beyond Impressionism: he uses color and style to create an immersive, imagined scene that asks us to think about something deeper.


*[READ: Steady, thoughtful, firm; give influence and harm equal clarity.]*

Gauguin's work shows both the possibilities and the consequences of painting from imagination. His flat shapes and invented colors influenced Matisse, Picasso, and later Symbolist artists. At the same time, the images carry a colonial fantasy that we need to recognize when we look at them.

*[READ: Natural pace, composed; gently gather the threads.]*

Of the four artists we discussed, Gauguin’s approach takes us furthest from direct observation. 
Now, let’s go back to the paintings we opened with and take another look at Post-Impressionism as a whole.

## ACT 6: FOUR ROADS INTO MODERN ART

*[READ: Lift gradually; brisk, warm, pleased to connect the ideas.]*

[FOUR-WAY SPLIT SCREEN: Seurat / Cézanne / Van Gogh / Gauguin]

[TALKING HEAD]

These four paintings still look nothing alike, and that is the point. Post-Impressionism was never one style. It was a later label for artists who were inspired by Impressionism and took it in different directions.

*[READ: Rhythmic, confident, engaged; give each artist equal space.]*

Seurat turned color into a system, while Cézanne used it to build structure. Van Gogh made it express emotion, and Gauguin used it to create symbolic, imagined worlds.

*[READ: Ease slightly; conversational, open, appreciative of the connections.]*

Obviously, sometimes their interests and explorations of art overlap. Some of these artists knew each other and inspired each other. But that’s what makes Post-Impressionism so interesting. These four artists are part of a wider movement that took the ideas of Impressionism in many different directions.

*[READ: Build gradually; expansive, excited; slow on “truly limitless”.]*

After Impressionism, there was no longer one clear answer for what art should be. It could study how we see, express inner experience, invent a world that had never existed, ask difficult questions, or simply be a provocation. Now artists had more and more freedom to decide what art could or should be — and the art world became truly limitless.

*[READ: Slow, intrigued, subtly uneasy; brighten on the final invitation.]*

Some artists were taking that freedom into stranger territory. Their art looks beautiful at first, but the longer you look, the more unsettling it becomes. Why did dreams and fears take these forms? And how did this strange world of images spread from paintings into posters, furniture, and entire buildings? That’s where we’re going next, with Symbolism and Art Nouveau.

### SCRIPT NOTES

**Central thesis:** Post-Impressionism was not one unified style. It was a set of divergent responses to the possibilities opened by Impressionism.

**Four-path framework:**

1. Seurat — system
2. Cézanne — structure
3. Van Gogh — emotion
4. Gauguin — dream, invention, and their ethical limits

**Tone arc:**

- Act 1: visual mystery, then conceptual clarity
- Seurat: precise and analytical
- Cézanne: patient, tactile, constructive
- Van Gogh: intimate but not sentimentalized
- Gauguin: visually attentive and ethically direct
- Conclusion: comparative, expansive, forward-looking

**Key visual comparisons:**

1. Monet / Seurat — spontaneous mark versus regulated mark
2. Three *Mont Sainte-Victoire* paintings — changes in constructed space
3. *The Potato Eaters* / Paris self-portrait — Van Gogh's palette transformation
4. Impressionist outdoor scene / *Vision After the Sermon* — observed light versus symbolic color
5. Four-way final comparison — system / structure / emotion / dream

**Important editorial principle:** Biography should explain an artistic decision or the conditions of an image. Do not add biographical detail merely because it is dramatic.

**Flow and delivery:** Each paragraph develops one connected idea. Artwork cues mark visual changes, not automatic pauses. Introduce each artist before the close analysis, then resolve the artistic problem before moving to the next artist. The comparisons between artists are an explanatory order, not a claim that each movement replaced the previous one.

**Standalone viewing:** Explain concepts and people when they first appear. No knowledge of another episode is assumed.
